Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithms

Ah, discrete logarithms, my favorite mathematical and computational problem. Now I know that people in cryptography and number theory will call me extremely boring for liking this problem, but I simply don't care. Now before I go on a rant on why I like this problem, it is important that you, the reader, to actually understand it. Now for this let's take another equally important problem in cryptography: Integer Factorization, this is basically the question that, it is easy to multiply 3 and 5 and then see that that equals 15, but how do we efficiently do the reverse? Based off of 15, how do I know what the factors are? This is a very good example of a one-way function. Now for discrete logarithms, it is somewhat similar. Here instead of having \(p*q=N\) and asking what \(p\) and \(q\) are, while only knowing \(N\), we are given \(Q=kG\) and try to recover \(k\). Now \(G\) is the generator point, this is a public point that is chosen together with the field or curve, which also creates a cyclic subgroup of large order (in most cases a large prime order). \(k\) is the secret scalar or in other words our private key, which is randomly generated. Now when we multiply \(k\) and \(G\) together, we get \(Q\), a public point, which also serves as our public key. The hard question now is, how do we get \(k\) while only knowing \(Q\), \(G\) and the field/curve? Now bruteforcing every possible point on the cyclic group costs \(O(n)\) in the worst case (where \(n\) is the order of the subgroup made by \(G\)).

There is a really cool algorithm developed by John Pollard (no, not the spy) which very cleverly achieves a complexity of \(O(\sqrt n)\), it performs a pseudorandom walk through the group using the birthday paradox to find collisions between previously found points, which in turn give us algebraic relations that allow us to recover \(k\) in \(O(\sqrt n)\) ops. This is a very crude explanation, which doesn't go into all the details, but as far as we know this is the best general-purpose algorithm for all discrete logarithms. There is also BSGS which deterministically recovers the discrete log in \(O(\sqrt n)\) but with way higher memory requirements than Pollard's algorithm(s). And then there is index calculus, and that is where things get really interesting. The rough idea uses the same general concept as NFS for integer factorization but applies it to our problem. It exploits certain properties of the group/curve and by doing that we can actually, very cheaply, generate large amounts of informative relations that decompose over a chosen factor base. This constrains the desired discrete logarithm value, allowing us to recover it in subexponential-time in some cases. Now this attack is very effective and elegant, however the problem is that you aren't always in a favorable regime where getting those relations is easy. Exactly because of that, a approach called Summation Polynomials was developed by Igor Semaev 22 years ago, transforming the bottleneck of relation generation into a point decomposition problem. Now for several months I have tried to tinker at these Point Decomposition Problems, but sadly the general case remains exponential and even proven to be NP-complete (with assumptions). So the quest after a new index calculus algorithm is still our northern star, but until we find a way to efficiently find informative relations that decompose over a small factor base, this still seems to be a hard problem.

Regular discrete logarithms over finite fields, usually have exploitable structure making index calculus applicable. However when the discrete logarithm is over a elliptic curve, things get harder, you lose the favorable notion of smoothness and factorization structure and thus the applicability of these efficient algorithms. The big exceptions of course is if the curve has other exploitable structures, like curves with extension fields, anomalous curves or some hyperelliptic curves/jacobians of growing genus. This is why for the most applications of elliptic curves in cryptography, we choose extremely large (256-bit) prime-field curves over \(F_p\), along with large embedding degrees to thwart MOV attacks. ECC was proposed in the mid 80s, by the 90s most of the major vulnerabilities were already discovered and the curves we use today, ensure that they feature none of these vulnerabilities. My question, for which I have gotten quite the pushback, is what happens if there is some structure that might have hidden beneath our noses that might just give an attacker an edge?

Another thing I should also mention, is that one could argue that in theory this problem is already solved. Since in 1994, a certain Peter Shor discovered that integer factorization as well as discrete logarithm problems naturally reduce to period finding problems which quantum computers could solve in polynomial-time (in this case very fast). Since then, there have been countless optimizations of this algorithm. My good friend Bartosz Naskręcki has actually made a lot of progress on that front, so if you are interested in these types of circuit optimization using AI research agents, I really recommend checking out his results over at https://ecdsa.fail/ ! Before I end this rant, I really want emphasize, just how far away we are from a machine that could run that algorithm for large instances. Until now the biggest number factored by Quantum Computers using Shor's algorithm is 15. We are decades away from a machine that is fault tolerant enough to run it. Heck, we can't even store the numbers on existing machines, let alone do arithmetic on them. Something I also noticed, is that because RSA has way larger key sizes, the arithmetic needed to perform Shor's on it will take way longer to be reliable on the machines than doing the EC arithmetic for breaking ECC. So while ECC has shorter keys, perhaps that wasn't such a good idea in the long run. I am betting that ECC will be broken before RSA using QCs.
