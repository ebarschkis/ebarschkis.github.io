About me

I was born in 2008 and grew up in Geneva, Switzerland. I attended and graduated from the German school of Geneva, which was the only German-speaking school in the entire region.

Up to the pandemic, I hadn't done many things that people would label as "interesting" or done anything remarkable.

I guess everything really started in 2020. The pandemic had just hit, and I was stuck at home with nothing to do all day. By then, I’d already grown fond of computers, mostly because I loved my brother’s video games he handed down to me. I loved taking apart my parents’ old PCs and laptops (sometimes without their approval) and scavenging parts just for the fun of it. Most of the time, I reassembled them and installed Linux onto them because Windows was running so painfully slow.

Soon enough, I became interested in puzzles, especially Kryptos, a collection of four to five ciphers etched into a copper sculpture at CIA headquarters in Langley, Virginia. I was hooked. I even found new ways to decipher the first three passages, though all three had already been cracked. Over time, I started meeting new people on forums, Discord servers, and even old IRC chatrooms. We collaborated together, interpreting clues and trying new ways to break the last unsolved passage. You can read more about those adventures in my Kryptos Notes (in the works).

Generally, the idea that a random guy like me could come together with people all over the world just to work on this one mysterious problem was fascinating to me. People from all walks of life, from Asia, South America, and even the far hinterlands of Siberia could collaborate on one single problem, just because they found it interesting. I kept working on Kryptos on and off for about five years, alongside school, Chemistry Olympiads, Student United Nations, and other projects.

Then, on September 5th, 2025, I woke up to my Kryptos friends in a frenzy. Two journalists had reportedly found the plaintext of the fourth passage in the Smithsonian archives. By that time, Kryptos was still a project I enjoyed, but the news ripped away much of the mystery for me. Over the following months, the big Kryptos group that I had been working with for all this time started to splinter into smaller factions. Some stuck with Kryptos, others moved to Eternity II (an edge-matching puzzle), the Zodiac ciphers, or hash preimages. I spent less and less time on Kryptos and joined the Eternity II and hash-preimage groups. After a few months, I got bored. Neither had the same sense of mystery for me and for a while I didn’t have much to do except boring schoolwork.

That was when I came across elliptic-curve cryptography and started nosing around the subject. Math wasn’t really my strong suit at the time, least of all computational number theory, but I naively thought that these were still just problems, and maybe, just maybe, I could help solve one. By late 2025, I was getting involved in the Erdős problems community, suggesting ideas, looking for relevant literature and testing the potential capability of frontier models in helping with these tasks. At the same time I was heavily working on elliptic-curve cryptography, specifically the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem, which has its own page. Around this time, I met people including Bartosz Naskrecki and Martin Shkreli, who were both deeply interested in the field.

January of 2026 was a pretty important month for me. I was taking my Abitur exams, which are pretty demanding high school leaving exams and while procrastinating studying for said exams, I kept working on Erdős problems. Five days before my first exam, I actually managed to solve one. With help from my friend Bartosz and Harmonic’s Aristotle formalization models, I quickly formalized the proof in Lean, a proof assistant and functional programming language. I posted it to the forum, where Terence Tao himself checked and validated the proof. Safe to say, I was absolutely ecstatic.

After my finals, I kept working on more Erdős problems. A few months passed with some improvements but no major breakthroughs. In March, I managed to produce yet another correct solution to another problem with the help of one of OpenAI’s top models that they provided to me. By then, Harmonic, the company founded by Vlad Tenev and Tudor Achim, had awarded me a research grant to continue working on mathematics with its new AI models.

By June, I had graduated from high school and turned 18. I’d also been invited to help design photonic chips in an initiative led by my friend and collaborator Martin Shkreli. The goal is to run LLMs orders of magnitude more efficiently than GPUs.
