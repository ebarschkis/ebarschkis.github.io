About me

Since you clicked here, I’m guessing you want (for whatever reason) to learn more about me.

I was born in 2008 and grew up in Geneva, Switzerland.

I guess everything really started in 2020. The pandemic had just hit, and I was stuck at home with nothing to do all day. By then, I’d already grown fond of computers. I loved taking apart my parents’ old machines (sometimes without their approval) and scavenging parts. Most of the time, I reassembled them and installed Ubuntu because Windows ran so slowly.

During the pandemic, I became interested in puzzles, especially Kryptos: the ciphers etched into a copper sculpture at CIA headquarters in Langley, Virginia. I was hooked. I even found new ways to decipher the first three passages, though all three had already been cracked.

Over time, I met people on forums, Discord servers, and even old IRC chatrooms. We worked together, interpreting clues and trying new ways to break the last unsolved passage. You can read more about those adventures in my Kryptos Notes (link coming soon).

I kept working on Kryptos on and off for about five years, alongside school, the Chemistry Olympiad, Student United Nations, and other projects.

On 5 September 2025, I woke up to my Kryptos friends in a panic. Two journalists had reportedly found the plaintext of the fourth passage in the Smithsonian archives. Kryptos was still a project I enjoyed, but the news took away much of the mystery for me.

Over the following months, the big Kryptos group started split into smaller teams. Some stuck with Kryptos; others moved to Eternity II (an edge-matching puzzle), the Zodiac ciphers, or hash preimages. I spent less time on Kryptos and joined the Eternity II and hash-preimage teams. After a few months, I got bored. Neither had the same sense of mystery for me.

That was when I came across Erdős problems and elliptic-curve cryptography. Math wasn’t really my strong suit at the time, but I naively thought that these were still problems, and maybe, just maybe, I could help solve one.

By late 2025, I was getting involved in the Erdős problems community: suggesting ideas, tracking down literature, and testing how much AI models had improved at mathematics. I was also working on elliptic-curve cryptography, specifically the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem, which has its own page (link coming soon). Around this time, I met people including Bartosz Naskrecki and Martin Shkreli.

January 2026 was a big month for me. I was taking my Abitur exams, which are fairly demanding high school leaving exams. To take a break from studying, I kept working on Erdős problems. Five days before my first exam, I solved one.

With help from my friend Bartosz and Harmonic’s Aristotle formalization models, I quickly formalized the proof in Lean, a proof assistant and functional programming language. I posted it to the forum, where Terence Tao himself checked and validated the proof. I was very happy that day, but I had to get back to studying for my finals.

After my finals, I kept working on more Erdős problems. A few months passed with some improvements but no major breakthroughs. In March, I managed to get one of OpenAI’s top models to produce a correct proof of another problem. By then, Harmonic had awarded me a research grant to continue working on mathematics with its new AI models.

By June, I had graduated from high school and turned 18. I’d also been invited to help design photonic chips in an initiative led by my friend and collaborator Martin Shkreli. The goal is to run LLMs orders of magnitude more efficiently than GPUs.
